SLIDE 1 — Title (0:00 - 0:15)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"Hi, we're going to walk through the observability stack and the traffic
engineering we built on top of Istio and the Bookinfo sample app — Kiali,
Grafana, and Jaeger, then canary releases and header-based routing for the
reviews service. We'll close with a short live demo."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 2 — Agenda (0:15 - 0:30)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"Three parts: first the observability tools we installed and what each one
is for; second, the two routing strategies we layered on the reviews
service; and finally a live demo where we apply both routing strategies and
watch the effect show up in real time in Kiali, Grafana, and Jaeger."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 3 — Mesh Architecture Under Observation (0:30 - 1:00)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"Quick refresher on the topology: traffic enters through the Istio ingress
gateway, hits productpage, which fans out to details and to reviews, and
reviews — depending on routing — calls ratings. Every one of those arrows
is actually a sidecar-to-sidecar Envoy call, which is exactly why we get
metrics, traces, and a topology graph for free without touching the
application code: Prometheus scrapes the sidecars, Jaeger collects the
spans they emit, and Kiali turns both into the graph you're about to see."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 4 — Kiali: Service Topology (1:00 - 1:40)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"Kiali gives us a live traffic graph, refreshed every 15 seconds straight
from Prometheus. Each edge shows request rate and success percentage, and
when we apply weighted or header-based routing rules, you literally see
the split happen on the graph — you can watch the canary edges to v1, v2,
v3 and the dedicated edge for the jason header light up. Kiali also has an
Istio Config view that lists every CRD we've applied — VirtualServices,
DestinationRules, the Gateway, PeerAuthentication, Telemetry — with a
validation status, which is how we sanity-checked every config in this
project before testing it."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 5 — Grafana: Metrics Dashboards (1:40 - 2:15)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"Grafana is fed by the same Prometheus instance. We installed a custom
Bookinfo dashboard with three panels: overall request rate per service,
the 5xx error rate, and — this is the one we'll use most today —
per-version request rate for reviews, so we can literally watch the 90/5/5
split happen in numbers. We also get the standard Istio addon dashboards
for the mesh, per-service, and control-plane metrics if we need to go
deeper."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 6 — Jaeger: Distributed Tracing (2:15 - 2:50)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"Jaeger gives us distributed tracing. We bumped trace sampling to 100% for
the bookinfo namespace via a Telemetry resource — Istio's default is only
1%, which is too sparse for a demo or for debugging. Because productpage
and reviews both propagate the W3C/B3 tracing headers onto their own
outbound calls, every request produces one connected trace across every
hop, and the search view here lists exactly that: every request entering
at productpage, fanning out to details and reviews."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 7 — Jaeger: Trace Waterfall (2:50 - 3:20)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"Drilling into one trace gives this waterfall: load generator into
productpage, fanning out to details and to reviews, and reviews calling
ratings — five services, twelve spans, each with its own timing. This is
the view that actually pinpoints which hop is slow or failing; in our
fault-injection work, for example, this is exactly where we could see the
delayed reviews-to-ratings span and the error icon bubble up to the
parent."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 8 — Traffic Engineering: Overview (3:20 - 3:40)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"Switching gears to traffic engineering. The goal is to control how
requests to reviews get distributed across v1, v2, v3 without touching a
single line of application code. We use two mechanisms, both expressed as
Istio VirtualServices: a weighted canary split, and header-based routing
for a specific user. And then we combine both into one VirtualService with
priority-ordered match rules."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 9 — Canary Release: 90/5/5 Split (3:40 - 4:05)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"This is the canary VirtualService: 90% of traffic to v1, 5% to v2, 5% to
v3 — a classic canary shape where the new versions only get a small
fraction of real traffic. We validate this automatically with
10-verify-canary.sh, which samples 100 requests and checks the combined
v2+v3 ratio lands within 10% plus or minus 5%."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 10 — Header-Based Routing (4:05 - 4:30)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"Layered on top of that is header-based routing. The combined
VirtualService has two ordered rules: first, if the request carries the
header end-user: jason, it goes 100% to reviews v2, deterministically.
Everything else falls through to the same 90/5/5 canary rule we just saw.
Order matters here — Istio evaluates HTTP match rules top to bottom and
takes the first match."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 11 — Routing Decision Path (4:30 - 4:50)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"Visually: every request to reviews asks one question — is this user
jason? If yes, it always lands on v2. If no, it falls into the canary
split. 11-test-header-routing.sh automates exactly this check: an
anonymous request should get any valid version, a jason request should
always get v2."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 12 — Observed Effect: Kiali Traffic Split (4:50 - 5:05)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"And here's what that looks like in Kiali once traffic is flowing: the
weighted edges to v1/v2/v3 plus the dedicated header-matched edge to v2,
all on the same graph. Let's go see this live."

----------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 13 — Live Demonstration (divider, 5:05 - 5:15)
----------------------------------------------------------------
"No more slides from here — we're going to the terminal and the browser.
Four things: confirm the mesh and observability stack are already up,
apply and verify the canary split, apply and verify header-based routing,
and watch both show up live in Kiali, Grafana, and Jaeger."

DEMO STEP 0 — Recap what's already running (5:15 - 5:35, ~20s)

SAY: "Lines 1 through 37 of run-all.sh are already done — I ran them before

DEMO STEP 1 — Apply the canary split (5:35 - 6:10, ~35s)
----------------------------------------------------------------
SAY: "First, the canary VirtualService — 90/5/5."
RUN:
  kubectl -n bookinfo apply -f networking/virtual-service-canary.yaml
SAY: "And we verify it automatically."
RUN:
  bash scripts/10-verify-canary.sh
SAY (while it runs / on result): "It samples 100 requests and confirms the
v2+v3 ratio is within tolerance of the configured 10%. PASS means the
split is live."

DEMO STEP 2 — Show the canary effect in Grafana and Kiali (6:10 - 7:00, ~50s)
----------------------------------------------------------------
DO: switch to the Grafana tab (start the port-forward if not already
running: `kubectl -n istio-system port-forward svc/grafana 3000:3000`).
SAY: "On the Bookinfo dashboard, per-version reviews request rate — you can
see v1 dominating with v2 and v3 trickling in at roughly 5% each."
DO: switch to the Kiali tab (`kubectl -n kiali port-forward svc/kiali
20001:20001`), open the traffic graph for the bookinfo namespace.
SAY: "And on the graph, the same thing — three weighted edges out of
reviews into the three subsets."

DEMO STEP 3 — Apply header-based routing (7:00 - 7:35, ~35s)
----------------------------------------------------------------
SAY: "Now let's layer in header-based routing for jason. We swap the
canary-only VirtualService for the combined one."
RUN:
  kubectl -n bookinfo delete virtualservice reviews-canary --ignore-not-found=true
  kubectl -n bookinfo apply -f networking/combined-virtual-service.yaml
SAY: "Then verify both paths."
RUN:
  bash scripts/11-test-header-routing.sh
SAY: "Anonymous requests still land on any valid version; a request with
end-user: jason always comes back as v2 — that's the PASS lines you're
seeing."

DEMO STEP 4 — Show the header-routing effect in Kiali and Jaeger (7:35 - 8:50, ~75s)
----------------------------------------------------------------
DO: refresh the Kiali traffic graph.
SAY: "Kiali now shows a fourth, header-matched edge straight to v2 for
jason traffic, on top of the same canary split for everyone else."
DO: log in to http://localhost:31080/productpage as jason (password
jason) to generate a couple of header-tagged requests, then switch to the
Jaeger tab (`kubectl -n jaeger port-forward svc/tracing 16686:80`).
SAY: "In Jaeger, searching for productpage traces, we can pick one of
jason's requests and see the full waterfall — productpage to reviews v2 to
ratings — confirming end to end which version actually served that
specific user, something the topology graph alone can't show you at the
per-request level."

DEMO STEP 5 — Wrap-up (8:50 - 9:10, ~20s)
----------------------------------------------------------------

Grafana, Jaeger — picked it up automatically, with zero changes to the
reviews or productpage code."
